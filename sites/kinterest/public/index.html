The bind

Pocket money is where they learn what money is worth. Most of it vanishes.

The cash disappears into pockets and into memory. Children's debit cards teach tapping, not thinking. And the IOUs in a parent's head — "I'll pay you back", "I'll sort it at the weekend" — are invisible to the child who is owed them.

Nobody sees the same picture. So nobody can plan, and nobody can learn from it. Kinterest puts the whole of it in one place — a ledger you can both open, not a number in one person's head.

How Kinterest works

Four promises, one shared ledger.

  1. 1

    One ledger, two signatures.

    You and your child look at the same ledger. Every entry is co-signed and can only be added to, never rewritten. No silent edits: a correction is a visible reversal, with a reason you can both read.

  2. 2

    They ask, you answer.

    Your child asks to spend. You see what for and how much, and you approve it — or not this time. Only then does the ledger update.

  3. 3

    Saving you can watch grow.

    You set the interest and pay it on a weekly interest day. Projections show the point of waiting — "leave it four more weeks and…" — and you can add a match on what they put in.

  4. 4

    No real money, ever.

    No card. No bank link. No payments. Not in the first version, not ever. It's a ledger: you hand over the actual money however you already do.

  • An interest day you set

    Pick the day of the week and the rate you're happy to pay. Interest lands on the same day every week, so it's something to look forward to.

  • Projections

    "Leave it four more weeks and…" — they see what waiting looks like before they decide to spend or keep.

  • An optional deposit match

    "Every £1 you save, I'll add 50p." Switch it on if you'd like to, leave it off if you wouldn't.

  • An account for each child

    Every child has their own balance, so two children can have two very different weeks.

  • Child mode on your phone

    Younger children don't need a phone. They can ask and look at their ledger on yours, in child mode.

  • Your controls, behind a PIN

    Approving, correcting and setting rates sit behind a separate PIN, and lock again the moment you leave the app.

  • No date of birth

    It never asks for one and never keeps one. A name and, if you choose, a contact photo — nothing more.

  • Encrypted family backup

    The whole family ledger is backed up encrypted, so a lost phone doesn't mean a lost history.

A week on the ledger.

  1. 1

    Hand it over. Pocket money, birthday money, a gift from Gran: it goes on the ledger, and the actual cash changes hands the way it always has.

  2. 2

    They ask. A book, a toy, a trip: Sam names it and the price, and you answer when you're ready.

  3. 3

    Interest day. On the day you chose, interest is added and the projections move. Waiting has a visible shape.

Interest is paid on what's on the ledger — which makes handing the cash over the sensible thing to do.

Questions

Straight answers.

Does it hold real money?

No. It's a ledger, not an account: no card, no bank link and no payments, now or later. You hand over the actual money however you already do — cash, a transfer, a birthday card — and the ledger records it.

Can I change an entry?

Only with a visible reversal and a reason. Entries are co-signed and can only be added to, so there are no silent edits — your child can see exactly what was corrected, and why.

Where does the interest come from?

From you. You set the rate and you pay it, on the interest day you choose. It's a lesson in what waiting looks like, not an investment — and the lesson matters more than the number.

Does my child need a phone?

No. Younger children can use child mode on yours, to ask and to look at their own ledger. Your controls stay behind a separate PIN.

What does it know about my child?

A name and, if you choose, a contact photo. No date of birth is asked for or kept.

Is it ready?

Not in the app stores yet, and there's no date to give you. The code is open on GitHub, so you can build the Android app yourself today.
